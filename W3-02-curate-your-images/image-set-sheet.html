How the style is held steady. Not by describing a mood. By fixing the numbers: paper #FAF9F7 background, #2E4B6B strokes at 2px, exactly one element in #C2410C and never more than one, no fills except dots, no gradients, no shadows, square line caps. Attempt one described the mood in words and generated all three at once, and gave me three stroke weights and two backgrounds.

Where a real capture beats AI

The case screenshots. These are the proof. A generated picture of a table of numbers is a picture of numbers I did not compute, on a site whose entire claim is that my numbers hold up. That is not a style preference, it is the argument. Rules for each: crop to the table, no browser chrome, no file paths or client names, light theme, 2x density so the digits survive.

The portrait. A real photo, shot against a plain wall in daylight and cropped to a circle. An AI portrait on a page arguing for honest evaluation is a joke that writes itself, and people can tell. It is live on the about page.

What I rejected

A generated "data scientist at work" hero. Person at a desk, three monitors, blue code glow. It looked competent and it was the easiest thing on the page to delete. It says nothing my one-line claim does not say better, and it makes a specific claim about a workspace that is not mine.

A generated neural network mesh. Glowing nodes, connecting lines. Rejected for a better reason than taste: it misdescribes the cases the hero sits above. I do build neural networks elsewhere and the about page links to that work, but these three cases are gradient boosting and cross-validation design. A hero makes a promise about the page underneath it, and the reader who came for deep learning leaves by paragraph two.

A logo with a shield or a checkmark. Tried it, because "numbers you can trust" pushes you toward trust badges. It looked like a security product. Claiming trustworthiness with an icon is the opposite of demonstrating it with a case study.

A generated portrait. Killed on sight.
