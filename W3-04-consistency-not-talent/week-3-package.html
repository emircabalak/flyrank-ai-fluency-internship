Week 3: three rounds of choosing

Emir Cabalak · General AI Fluency · Consistency, Not Talent (and Frame, Not Upstage)

The week-3 package in one document: the one-line claim, the content map with its gather list, the identity kit, and the image set. I have arranged it around what the session was about rather than around the three cards, because the same move happens three times. Generate many, keep one, be able to say why the others died. Roughly twenty options were produced across the week and four survived. What follows is mostly the rejections, since that is where the judgment is visible.

Round one, the words

Ten claims generated, one kept.

I build machine learning models and then try to break my own results before anyone else can.

The cuts, and the rule each one taught me.

Rigorous, reproducible ML evaluation for data teams. Dead on arrival. That is the vocabulary of somebody who has never had to defend a number in a meeting, and it would sit unchanged on any consultancy page.

Turning messy search data into models that do not lie to you. Dies on "messy", which describes everyone's data and therefore describes nobody's.

Machine learning you can check. Also honest evaluation for models that have to survive production. True and forgettable. Both state a category rather than a behaviour, and nobody repeats a category.

I care more about the split than the algorithm. The sharpest line on the list and I nearly took it. Cut because it is an opinion about the field, not a claim about me. Good to say once somebody is already listening, bad as a first sentence.

ML engineer who reports the lower number. Closest runner up. Cut because "the lower number" needs a paragraph of setup, and the first sentence does not get one.

I find the leak in the pipeline, usually my own. The most human line here, and it undersells. It makes me sound like an auditor of other people's work. I build the model too and the claim has to say so.

The keeper. It names both halves of the job, building and breaking. "Break my own results" is specific and slightly uncomfortable, which is what makes it stick, and "before anyone else can" tells a reader what problem it solves for them. It could not be pasted onto somebody else's portfolio without becoming false, which is the only test that matters.

One sharpening pass after that. "ML" became "machine learning", because the first sentence on a page should not need an acronym. "Someone else does" became "anyone else can". And "try to" stayed, even though "and break" is punchier, because I do not always succeed and the whole page argues against overstating.

Round two, the frame

The design frames the work and never upstages it. Here is what that cost.

Headings: Space Grotesk

Weights 500 and 600. Free, Google Fonts.

I break my own results

Body: IBM Plex Sans

Weights 400 and 600. Drawn for technical documentation, so it survives a page full of digits.

ROC AUC dropped from 0.91 to 0.84 once the leaking pair came out, and 0.84 is the number I report.

What I cut. A third face, IBM Plex Mono, for the metric tables. It would have looked right. Three fonts is a pile, and font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums on Plex Sans aligns the digits without another download, which covers the actual reason I wanted mono.

The mark I rejected. A shield, then a checkmark. "Numbers you can trust" pushes you straight at trust badges, and both attempts looked like a security product. Claiming trustworthiness with an icon is the opposite of demonstrating it with a case study, so the monogram stayed plain and lowercase. EC in a box reads as a corporate badge; this is a person's site.

Fonts: Space Grotesk for headings, IBM Plex Sans for body. Colors: paper #FAF9F7, ink #16181D, main #2E4B6B, accent #C2410C, dark mode variants in tokens.css. Accent is reserved for the single call to action.

Mood: a lab notebook, not a brochure. Plain, quiet, and slightly technical, so the numbers and the writing are the loudest thing on the page. Generous white space, one column, no gradients, no shadows, no animation.

Why quiet is not a taste preference here. My claim is that my numbers hold up when somebody checks them. A page with gradients and motion undercuts that before a reader reaches a sentence, because it is visibly trying to impress and the whole argument is that I am not. That is the frame-not-upstage rule with something actually at stake: the design is making the same claim as the text, or it is arguing against it.

How the style is held steady. Not by describing a mood. By fixing the numbers: paper background, #2E4B6B strokes at two pixels, exactly one element in #C2410C and never more than one, no fills except the dots, no gradients, square line caps. Attempt one described the mood in words and generated all three at once, and produced three stroke weights and two different backgrounds. Describing a mood does not hold a style. Fixing the numbers does.

portrait.jpg

Where a real capture beats anything generated. The three case screenshots are the proof. A generated picture of a table of numbers is a picture of numbers I did not compute, on a site whose entire claim is that my numbers hold up. That is not a style preference, it is the argument. Rules for each: crop to the table, no browser chrome, no file paths or client names, light theme, 2x density so the digits survive.

The portrait is a real photograph. Plain wall, daylight, one frame, cropped to a circle. An AI portrait on a page arguing for honest evaluation is a joke that writes itself, and people can tell.

What I rejected, and why each one died

The part that is actually graded.

A generated "data scientist at work" hero. Person at a desk, three monitors, blue code glow, the whole stock-photo grammar. It looked competent and it was the easiest thing on the page to delete. It says nothing my one-line claim does not say better, it makes a specific claim about a workspace that is not mine, and it sat in the most valuable space on the site doing decoration.

A generated neural network mesh. Glowing nodes and connecting lines. Rejected for a better reason than taste: it misdescribes the cases the hero sits above. I do build neural networks elsewhere and the about page links to that work, but these three cases are gradient boosting and cross-validation design. A hero makes a promise about the page underneath it, and the reader who came for deep learning leaves by paragraph two. An image that misdescribes the work is worse than no image.

A generated portrait. Killed on sight, for the reason above.

A shield, and then a checkmark, in the logo. Looked like a security product both times.

A third typeface. Wanted, justified, and still cut.

The pattern. Four of the five died for the same reason, and it is not "I liked the other one better". Each made a promise the page underneath could not keep. That is the only rejection test I ended up needing, and it works on words, on colour and on images equally.
